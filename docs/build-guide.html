Design decisions what changed from the first sketch, and why

Every item here replaced something that would not have worked. They are recorded because the reasoning is more reusable than the conclusion, and because two of them are the same argument applied twice.

The heater is 12 V DC, not a mains cable

Zoo Med Repti Heat Cable, 120 VAC → 12 V DC silicone cable, 20 W

The original parts list paired a wall-plug mains heat cable with an IRLZ44N MOSFET. A logic-level DC MOSFET cannot switch mains at all, so the design would not have run — and building it anyway would have put 120 V inside a plastic box alongside water lines. At 12 V one supply feeds the heater and both pumps, and the cable wraps the outside of the nest, so it needs no seal, never gets wet, and no ant can reach it.

No gate between nest and outworld

servo gate + IR interlock → three always-open risers

A powered gate was the largest single point of failure in the system. A servo that jams closed cuts the colony off from food and water, unattended, for as long as it takes someone to notice — and no firmware can detect the difference between a gate that is closed and a gate that is stuck. Three risers are also redundant against one fouling with debris or a dead ant. Closure for servicing is a manual plug cap, which cannot fail shut while nobody is watching.

The pump fills a reservoir, not the nest

probe-driven misting into substrate → pump → 131 mL reservoir → wicking

The same argument, pointed at what was left. With the gate gone the mist pump became the biggest remaining single point of failure, and a maximum-runtime limit in firmware is no defence against the failures that actually flood a sealed nest: a MOSFET that fails short, a pump head that jams open, a reservoir siphoning through a stopped pump. So the pump was moved off the nest entirely. It fills a reservoir smaller than a harmful dose, and the reservoir wicks into the Ytong core with no actuator involved. The guarantee moved out of the code and into the geometry, where an electrical fault cannot reach it.

Square section, not a cylinder

6 in ID cast acrylic tube → ¼ in flat sheet, four walls

An O-ring cannot seat on a curve, and a riser welded to a curved wall cannot be clamped while it cures — so every joint that carries containment wanted a flat face. Three other things fell out for free: large-bore cast acrylic tube is a specialty cut-to-order item and was the most expensive part in the build, while flat sheet is a commodity; the Ytong core becomes a saw cut instead of a turning job; and flat faces do not distort the camera. The cost is four longitudinal seams where a tube had none — but flat-panel solvent welding is how every acrylic aquarium is built, and those hold water. This one only has to hold ants.

An opaque sleeve over the nest

bare clear acrylic nest → removable opaque sleeve

A clear nest is permanently lit by room light. Ants dig away from light, so the original design would have produced both chronic stress and a nest you could not see into — they would have tunnelled against whichever face was darkest. Darkness by default, full view on demand. Borrowed from the way Tar Heel Ants cover their nests.

Two humidity sensors, and a capped expansion port

SHT31 at 0x44 and 0x45 · bulkhead port, plugged

The SHT31's ADDR pin gives two addresses on one bus, so one extra part buys a reading in each zone: outworld air, which is what the fan can act on, and nest air, where mould risk actually lives. Detecting a wet-substrate / dry-air mismatch — an original design goal — needs both. Separately, a capped port goes into the nest wall now, while the box is empty: Tetramorium was chosen because it reaches 1,000–10,000 workers, and cutting into a populated column later is not a plan.

An IP camera, not an ESP32-CAM

ESP32-CAM + 850 nm illuminator → IR ONVIF/RTSP camera

.NET nanoFramework has no OV2640 driver — an open feature request with no library — so that board would have meant one Arduino C++ component in an otherwise all-C# project. An off-the-shelf IR camera needs no firmware at all, and 850 nm is invisible to the ants and to us, so the column can be watched all night without lighting it.

Three ways to lose a day

ADC2 is dead while WiFi is on. The soil probe must be on ADC1 (GPIO 32–36, 39). This is the most common ESP32 sensor bug and it presents as a sensor that worked on the bench.

nanoFramework's 1-Wire runs over a UART, not a bit-banged pin. It needs a COM port's RX and TX tied together to the DQ line, with a 4.7 kΩ pull-up to 3V3. Without the pull-up the bus reads all-ones and every probe enumerates as absent.

Record the DS18B20 ROM IDs explicitly. Do not rely on enumeration order. Getting it wrong is silent: the controller heats to the outworld probe and cooks the nest, or heats to the nest-top probe and never reaches setpoint. Neither looks like a wiring fault.

Why the float switch reads LOW for full

The switch is wired to ground and read against the internal pull-up, so a risen float reads LOW. That direction is chosen, not convenient. A broken wire floats HIGH, which reads not full, which asks for a refill — and that refill is bounded twice over, by the runtime limiter and by the fact that the reservoir cannot physically hold a harmful dose. The opposite polarity would read full forever on a broken wire, silently stop hydrating the colony, and look perfectly healthy on the dashboard. Fail-safe here means failing toward a bounded action, because doing nothing is what kills a colony slowly.

Before the colony arrives

Run the whole column empty for two weeks. Confirm the nest holds 79–83 °F overnight, that a refill cycle completes and the reservoir reads full, that the substrate stabilises rather than trending, and that no fault has latched. Order the colony only after that. The failures that matter in this system are slow ones, and two weeks is the shortest window in which a slow failure becomes visible.

The experiment

Each riser mouth is lit from below through a frosted ring-window in the outworld floor, in one of three channels, at a brightness that tracks that riser's own traffic. Traffic changes the light at the choice point; the light may change the traffic.

What it actually measures

Ants are most sensitive to UV and blue-green and are effectively blind to deep red. So these are not three flavours of one stimulus — they are three very different ones. The prediction is negative feedback on blue, weak feedback on green, and almost none on red, which means traffic should drift toward whichever riser is currently showing red.

Red — near-invisible, so near-zero deterrence
Green — visible, moderate deterrence
Blue — highly visible, strongest deterrence

Three conditions, and one control that makes it mean anything

  • Off — dark. Traffic logged with no light at all: the baseline.
  • Fixed — every ring lit equally. Colour present, carrying no information about traffic. This is what separates "ants respond to colour" from "ants respond to light changing at all".
  • Closed loop — the treatment.

The load-bearing control is the rotation: the riser-to-channel mapping advances at local midnight on a three-day cycle, and every traffic row in the log carries the channel and brightness that were in force when it was counted. Without it, "they prefer green" and "they prefer the tube nearest the food dish" produce identical data and the experiment means nothing. The dashboard shows both groupings side by side for exactly this reason.

A guaranteed dark period runs 22:00–07:00 local regardless of mode or activity, so the colony always has the option of darkness.
